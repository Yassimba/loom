# Explanation rules

Explanation is a **discussion**: reflective, understanding-oriented treatment of a bounded topic. It gives the reader context, makes connections, and answers “Can you tell me about…?” or “Why is it this way?”

It should make sense away from the product and outside the pressure of completing a task.

## Bound the topic

Explanation has no natural endpoint: a topic can always connect to something else. Establish a meaningful boundary before writing.

Use a real or imagined “why” question to control the scope. Shape the title so it works after an implicit **About**, for example “About authentication” or “Why connection policies use backoff.” Labels such as “Background,” “Concepts,” or “Discussion” may identify the same kind of document.

The topic, audience, and central question together determine what belongs. Resist absorbing every related instruction or technical fact merely because it touches the subject.

## Build understanding

A strong explanation unfolds what facts and procedures leave implicit:

- connect the topic to adjacent parts of the system and ideas the reader already understands;
- supply historical, organizational, or technical context;
- explain design decisions, causes, constraints, and consequences;
- compare choices, alternatives, and possibilities;
- use examples and analogies to reveal relationships;
- describe internal interactions when they clarify why the system behaves as it does;
- draw implications rather than leaving facts isolated.

Understanding is a web. Each connection should help the reader place previously separate knowledge into a coherent picture.

## Admit perspective

Explanation may contain reasoned judgement, interpretation, and opinion. Mark perspective as perspective rather than presenting it as neutral fact.

Consider counterexamples, trade-offs, competing approaches, and the conditions under which another choice would be reasonable. “It depends” is useful when followed by what it depends on.

Explanation is discussion, not advocacy. The purpose is to open the topic for informed consideration.

## Protect the boundary

Keep the reader thinking rather than doing.

- Move ordered actions toward a goal into a how-to guide.
- Move guided learning activity into a tutorial.
- Move accumulating signatures, options, and raw facts into reference.

Brief facts or examples may support the discussion, but should not overtake it. Link to the appropriate document when another need arises.

## Language

Use language that exposes relationships and reasoning:

- “The reason for X is…” explains causes.
- “X was chosen over Y because…” gives a judgement and its grounds.
- “X is analogous to Y, except…” connects the unfamiliar to the familiar without hiding differences.
- “Some teams prefer X when…” considers alternatives and conditions.
- “X interacts with Y by…” unfolds behavior that would otherwise remain opaque.

## Completion

The explanation is done when a reader matching the agreed audience can answer the controlling “why” question, relate the topic to its relevant context, and understand the significant choices and trade-offs without the document escaping its stated boundary.
