# Tutorial rules

A tutorial is a **lesson**: a guided, practical learning experience in which a newcomer acquires skill through meaningful action. The artifact they build is the vehicle for learning, not the point of the lesson.

## Teacher’s contract

Take responsibility for the learner’s experience. Assume only that they will pay attention and follow clear directions; never transfer responsibility for success, understanding, or recovery to them.

Design an experience that is:

- **Meaningful:** it gives the learner a recognizable achievement.
- **Successful:** the agreed audience can complete it reliably.
- **Logical:** each action prepares for what follows.
- **Usefully complete:** it encounters the actions, concepts, and tools the learner needs for this lesson.

Written instruction must compensate for the absent teacher. Anticipate failure, state what should happen, and test with representative newcomers. A command that usually works is not reliable enough for a tutorial.

## Shape the learning experience

1. **Show the destination.** Open with the concrete result the learner will produce. Say “In this tutorial, we will build…” rather than claiming what they will learn.
2. **Produce a result early.** Reduce the time between an unfamiliar action and its visible effect.
3. **Maintain a narrative of expectations.** After every consequential action, show the expected result or output. Warn about surprising output and name likely signs of a mistake with a recovery action.
4. **Direct attention.** Point out changes and details the learner may miss while concentrating on the steps. Observation is part of the skill.
5. **Build a feeling of doing.** Arrange purpose, action, thought, and result into a coherent rhythm rather than a collection of commands.
6. **Permit repetition.** Prefer reversible or repeatable exercises so the learner can reproduce a successful result and build confidence.
7. **Move from concrete to abstract.** Keep the learner working with this problem, action, and result. Let broader patterns emerge from concrete experience.
8. **Give one path.** Choose one verified route to success. Move options, alternatives, and surveys of the feature space into how-to or reference material.

## Control explanation

Explanation competes with the learner’s attention. Include only the immediate reason needed to keep an action intelligible, usually one sentence, and link to a separate explanation for more.

Every paragraph should either:

- prompt an action;
- prepare the learner for its result;
- confirm the observed result; or
- point out something relevant in the result.

Move background, exhaustive facts, choices, and conceptual discussion to their proper quadrants.

## Language

Use the first-person plural to make the teacher–learner relationship explicit:

- “In this tutorial, we will…” states the destination.
- “First, do X. Now do Y.” leaves no uncertainty about sequence.
- “We do X before Y because…” gives only the rationale needed now.
- “The output should look like…” establishes a checkpoint.
- “Notice that…”, “Let’s check…”, and “You should now see…” direct attention and confirm progress.
- “We have built…” closes by naming the learner’s achievement.

Avoid “You will learn…”: promise the experience and result, not an internal outcome you cannot observe.

## Completion

The tutorial is done when a newcomer matching the agreed audience can follow it from start to finish, achieve the promised meaningful result, and confirm progress at every consequential step. Observe representative users when possible to find gaps the author cannot see.
