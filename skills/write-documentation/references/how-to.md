# How-to guide rules

A how-to guide is a **recipe**: practical direction that helps an already-competent reader solve a real-world problem or reach a specific result correctly and safely.

## Start from a human project

Define the guide by the reader’s goal, not by a feature or operation the product happens to expose. Tools are means within the reader’s project; the project determines where the guide starts, what it crosses, and where it ends.

Test the proposed subject:

- It names a result, task, or problem a reader actually encounters.
- The reader already knows what they want and has the competence to act on the guidance.
- It is bounded enough to guide. “How to build a web application” is a field of skill, not a specific goal.
- It adds more than an obvious interface label or a restatement of what a control does.

Title the guide **How to <achieve X>**. If the title needs “and” to join separate outcomes, write two guides. Prefer a precise goal-bearing title over a topic label such as “Application monitoring.”

## Guide action

Open with the result and the preconditions needed to begin, then enter the actions directly.

- Keep every instruction in service of the stated goal.
- Use an executable, logically ordered sequence. Put an action first when it enables either the next operation or the reader’s thinking for what follows.
- Include judgement and decision-making where the problem requires them; useful guidance is not always a single linear procedure.
- Address meaningful branches, overlaps, and entry or exit points. Real work can require conditional paths rather than an artificially clean sequence.
- Make the guidance adaptable to nearby real-world cases without attempting to enumerate every possible case.
- Start and stop at reasonable points. The competent reader can join the guide to the rest of their own work.

## Seek flow

Order the guide around the reader’s activity and train of thought, not merely around the product’s internal sequence.

Check for friction:

- unnecessary switching between tools or contexts;
- unresolved information the reader must hold for too long;
- later steps that force them to reconsider earlier decisions;
- erratic pacing or interruptions that could be removed;
- prerequisites revealed only after work has begun.

A strong guide anticipates the next thing the reader needs and presents it at that moment.

## Keep the guide pure

Practical usability matters more than completeness. Omit material that does not help achieve the goal.

- Teaching foundational skill belongs in a tutorial.
- Reasons, history, and conceptual discussion belong in explanation.
- Exhaustive options and product facts belong in reference.

Link to those documents at the point where they become useful instead of embedding them. Do not turn the guide into a feature tour or a complete account of the machinery.

## Language

Use direct and conditional imperatives:

- “This guide shows you how to…” defines the problem or result.
- “To achieve X, do Y.” ties an action to its purpose.
- “If your deployment uses X, do Y; otherwise, continue with Z.” handles a real branch.
- “For every available option, see…” links to reference without interrupting the task.

## Completion

The guide is done when the stated preconditions are sufficient, each relevant real-world branch leads to an explicit next action, and a competent reader can achieve the named result without needing an unstated step.
